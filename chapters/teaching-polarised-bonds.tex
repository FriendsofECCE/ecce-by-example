\chapter{Polarised bonds and non-bonding orbitals}
\label{ch:t-polarised-bonds}
\index{Polar bond}
\index{Molecular orbitals!polarised}
\index{Non-bonding orbital}
\index{Hydrogen fluoride}\index{Carbon monoxide}\index{Lithium hydride}
\eccexample{Examples > General Chemistry > Molecular orbitals of diatomics}

In \chem{H2} the bonding orbital is shared equally by the two atoms. Between two different atoms it is not, and the orbital pictures show it directly: the bonding orbital is bigger on one atom than on the other. This chapter gives the teacher three molecules in which to see where the electrons of a bond sit, \chem{HF}, where fluorine takes most of the bond, \chem{LiH}, where hydrogen is the negative end, and \chem{CO}, whose highest occupied orbital is a lone pair on the less electronegative atom. It also shows a class orbitals that belong to one atom only, the non-bonding orbitals, which become the lone pairs of a Lewis structure.

\section*{This requires}
Nothing beyond ECCE: the calculations are in the General Chemistry example set.

\section{The calculations}
The topic \menu{Molecular orbitals of diatomics} holds \emph{HF}, \emph{LiH} and \emph{CO}, each one molecule in the gas phase at its optimised structure. For each orbital, table~\ref{tab:pb} gives its energy and how much of it sits on each atom, worked out from the stored orbitals: 100\,\% on one atom means that the orbital belongs to that atom alone, 50\,\% on each means equal sharing. Energies are in electronvolts (eV); the more negative the energy, the more tightly the orbital holds its electrons.

Figure~\ref{fig:pb-diagrams} shows that the occupied bonding level of \chem{HF} lies close to the fluorine 2p level and far below hydrogen 1s, while that of \chem{LiH} lies close to hydrogen 1s and far below lithium 2s. This is because a bonding orbital is built mostly from the lower of the two atomic levels it combines, and the lower level belongs to the atom that holds its electrons more tightly, the more electronegative one: fluorine in \chem{HF}, hydrogen in \chem{LiH}. Hence the diagram tells the class at a glance which atom gets the larger share of the bonding electrons, and that hydrogen is the poorer partner in one molecule and the richer one in the other. In \chem{HF} the two levels at the energy of fluorine 2p marked \texttt{nb} have no partner on hydrogen at all; they are the non-bonding orbitals of the next section.

\section{Hydrogen fluoride}
In \chem{HF} the bonding orbital sits 85\,\% on fluorine and only 15\,\% on hydrogen, and its surface (figure~\ref{fig:pb-orbitals}) is centred on fluorine: a large lobe on the far side of F and a second lobe, of the other sign, that runs from F over H. This shows that the shared pair of the H--F bond is shared unequally, because the fluorine 2p orbital lies far lower in energy than the hydrogen 1s orbital and the bonding orbital takes most of its character from the lower one. Hence the electrons of the bond spend most of their time near fluorine, which is the negative end of the molecule: the dipole moment is 1.86~D with F negative. The empty $\sigma^*$ orbital is the mirror image, 68\,\% on hydrogen.

The two highest occupied orbitals of \chem{HF} lie entirely on fluorine; the picture shows one of them as a p orbital on F standing across the bond, with nothing on H. They are the 2p orbitals of fluorine that point across the bond, and hydrogen has no orbital of the right shape to combine with them: its 1s orbital overlaps with the positive lobe of such a p orbital as much as with the negative one, and the two cancel. Hence these orbitals neither bond nor antibond; they are non-bonding, and in the Lewis structure they are two of the three lone pairs on fluorine.
\index{Non-bonding orbital!HF}

\section{Lithium hydride}
In \chem{LiH} the bonding orbital leans the other way, 55\,\% on hydrogen and 45\,\% on lithium, and the empty orbital above it is almost entirely lithium; in the picture the bonding orbital is a cloud around hydrogen. This shows that hydrogen is the more electronegative partner here, because the lithium 2s electron is held more loosely than the hydrogen 1s electron. Hence the negative end of \chem{LiH} is the hydrogen atom: the molecule is a hydride, with a dipole moment of 5.63~D pointing from H to Li. The pair \chem{HF} and \chem{LiH} shows a class that the direction of a bond's polarity is not a property of hydrogen but of its partner.

\section{Carbon monoxide}
In \chem{CO} the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonding orbitals sit about 80\,\% on oxygen, as expected for the more electronegative atom. The highest occupied orbital, however, sits 87\,\% on carbon and points away from oxygen along the axis: it is the lone pair on carbon of the Lewis structure :C$\equiv$O:, and in its picture the large lobe sticks out from carbon on the side away from oxygen. This shows that the electrons a reaction partner meets first in \chem{CO} are on carbon, because the highest occupied orbital is the one most easily given away. Hence \chem{CO} binds to metals through carbon, as in metal carbonyls and in carboxyhaemoglobin. The empty $\pi^*$ orbitals are also larger on carbon, 65\,\%, so carbon is the end that both gives and accepts electrons.
\index{Carbon monoxide!lone pair on carbon}

The polar bonds and the lone pair on carbon pull in opposite directions, and the dipole moment of \chem{CO} is small: 0.06~D calculated, 0.11~D measured in the gas phase \cite{cccbdb}, against 1.86~D for \chem{HF}. In the calculation the negative end is carbon.

\section{In class}
Draw the bonding orbital of \chem{HF} and of \chem{H2} (Chapter~\ref{ch:t-bond-order}) next to each other: the class sees the shared pair shift toward fluorine. Then draw the highest occupied orbital of \chem{CO} and ask which atom a metal ion would bind to.

\section{History}
Mulliken's electronegativity scale of 1934 measures how strongly an atom holds its electrons by the mean of its ionisation energy and electron affinity \cite{mulliken1934}.
