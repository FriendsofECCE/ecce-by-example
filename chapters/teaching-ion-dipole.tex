\chapter{Ion-dipole interactions}
\label{ch:t-ion-dipole}
\index{Ion--dipole interaction}
\index{Sodium ion}
\index{Intermolecular interactions}
\eccexample{Examples > General Chemistry > Intermolecular interactions}

A sodium ion in water is surrounded by water molecules that turn their oxygen ends toward it. This chapter gives the teacher a picture of that attraction and a measure of its strength, taken from stored calculations of one Na$^+$ with one water molecule, the smallest case of an ion--dipole interaction. The NaCl ion pair of the same topic is the contrast, two ions instead of an ion and a dipole, and the comparison shows a class why ions bind water so strongly and why the attraction between two ions reaches so much further.

\section*{This requires}
Nothing beyond ECCE: the calculations are in the General Chemistry example set.

\section{The calculation}
The topic \menu{Intermolecular interactions} holds \emph{na\_h2o}, one Na$^+$ with one water molecule, and \emph{nacl\_ion\_pair}, both optimised in ECCE. For the curves of figure~\ref{fig:id-curve} the Na--O and Na--Cl distances were scanned (a relaxed scan, Chapter~\ref{ch:scans}): ECCE held the distance fixed at a series of values, from a little inside the optimised distance out to \SI{10}{\angstrom}, and optimised everything else at each value. Each point of a curve is the energy of the pair at that distance relative to the two partners far apart, Na$^+$ and H$_2$O, or Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$, each calculated on its own. A negative value means that the pair is held together by that much energy.

\section{Orientation}
The structure shows which end of the water molecule the ion attracts. The sodium ion sits on the twofold axis of the water molecule, on the oxygen side, \SI{2.21}{\angstrom} from O, and both hydrogens point away from it, with Na--O--H \ang{127.4}. The charge that ECCE assigns to Na (its Mulliken charge) is $+0.95$, so the ion keeps almost all of its charge and shares hardly any electrons with the water. The molecule turns its negative end, the oxygen with its two lone pairs, toward the cation because the positive charge attracts the negative end of the dipole and repels the positive one. Hence the picture of hydrated Na$^+$ in a textbook, with oxygen atoms pointing inward, follows from the attraction of a charge for a dipole alone.

\section{The curve}
The curve answers how strongly one water molecule is held and how far the attraction reaches. Its lowest point is at Na--O \SI{2.21}{\angstrom}, the optimised structure, \SI{113.2}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated partners (table~\ref{tab:id-curve}; kJ/mol is the energy per mole of pairs). That is more than four times the \SI{25.9}{kJ.mol^{-1}} of the hydrogen bond in the water dimer of Chapter~\ref{sec:hydrogen-bond}, calculated in the same way, because the full charge of the ion pulls on the water dipole, while in a hydrogen bond one dipole pulls on another. The attraction also falls off slowly: at \SI{5}{\angstrom} the pair is still \SI{25.8}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated partners, as much as a whole hydrogen bond, and at \SI{10}{\angstrom} still \SI{6.0}{kJ.mol^{-1}}. Doubling the distance from 5 to \SI{10}{\angstrom} divides the energy by four, the $R^{-2}$ fall-off of a charge attracting a dipole. Hence an ion holds not only the water molecules that touch it but also, more weakly, those further out, which is why ions in water carry a shell of oriented water molecules with them.

\section{Two ions}
The NaCl ion pair shows what changes when the water molecule is replaced by a second ion. Its lowest point is at Na--Cl \SI{2.36}{\angstrom}, \SI{591.5}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated ions, five times the ion--dipole well, because the chloride ion brings a whole negative charge where water brings only the negative end of a dipole. The attraction between two charges falls off as $R^{-1}$, more slowly than the $R^{-2}$ of a charge and a dipole. The dotted reference curve in figure~\ref{fig:id-curve} shows this for two whole charges, $-1389/R$~kJ/mol with $R$ in \AA: it is still \SI{-139}{kJ.mol^{-1}} at \SI{10}{\angstrom}, more than the whole ion--dipole well. Hence two ions attract each other strongly over distances where an ion and a water molecule hardly notice each other, which is why salts have high melting points.

The calculated curve does not rise towards the zero line at large distance, and the teacher should not read its tail as an attraction. The zero is two whole ions, each calculated on its own. The pair, however, is one calculation, and as the ions move apart some electron density flows back from Cl$^-$ to Na$^+$: at \SI{9.96}{\angstrom} the charges are only $\pm0.56$, so the far end of the curve is neither two ions nor two atoms. Two neutral atoms lie \SI{199}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below two ions (the dash-dotted line), because taking the electron from Na costs more energy than Cl gains by taking it, so far apart in the gas phase the pair would rather be two atoms. The curve is therefore a picture of the ion pair near its minimum, and its far end says nothing about how strongly two ions attract each other; for that the dotted Coulomb curve is the one to show.

\section{History}
Coulomb measured the force between two charged spheres with his torsion balance and reported in 1785 that it falls off as the inverse square of their distance \cite{coulomb1785}; the energy of two ions, $-1389/R$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ for whole charges, follows from his law. Debye introduced the permanent dipole moment of a molecule in 1912 to explain how the dielectric constant of a polar substance depends on temperature \cite{debye1912}, and collected the theory in his book \emph{Polar Molecules} of 1929 \cite{debye1929}. Born treated a hydrated ion in 1920 as a charged sphere in a continuous dielectric and obtained the hydration energy of the ion from its radius and the dielectric constant of water \cite{born1920}; the calculation of this chapter looks at the same attraction one water molecule at a time.
