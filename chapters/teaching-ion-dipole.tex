\chapter{Ion-dipole interactions}
\label{ch:t-ion-dipole}
\index{Ion--dipole interaction}
\index{Sodium ion}
\index{Intermolecular interactions}
\eccexample{Examples > General Chemistry > Intermolecular interactions}

A sodium ion in water is surrounded by water molecules that turn their oxygen ends toward it. This chapter gives the teacher a picture of that attraction and a measure of its strength, taken from stored calculations of one Na$^+$ with one water molecule, the smallest case of an ion--dipole interaction. The NaCl ion pair of the same topic is the contrast, two ions instead of an ion and a dipole, and the comparison shows a class how strongly an ion holds the water molecule next to it and why the attraction between two ions reaches so much further.

\section*{This requires}
Nothing beyond ECCE: the calculations are in the General Chemistry example set.

\section{The calculation}
The topic \menu{Intermolecular interactions} holds \emph{na\_h2o}, one Na$^+$ with one water molecule, and \emph{nacl\_ion\_pair}, both optimised in ECCE. For the curves of figure~\ref{fig:id-curve} the Na--O and Na--Cl distances were scanned, starting from these two structures (a relaxed scan, Chapter~\ref{ch:scans}): ECCE held the distance fixed at a series of values, from about \SI{2}{\angstrom} out to \SI{10}{\angstrom}, and optimised everything else at each value. The curves are for the ions in water, because that is where salt is in a classroom. Each point of a curve is the energy of the pair at that distance relative to the two partners far apart, Na$^+$ and H$_2$O, or Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$, each calculated on its own in water. A negative value means that the pair is held together by that much energy.

\section{Orientation}
The structure shows which end of the water molecule the ion attracts. The sodium ion sits on the twofold axis of the water molecule, on the oxygen side, \SI{2.21}{\angstrom} from O, and both hydrogens point away from it, with Na--O--H \ang{127.4}. The charge that ECCE assigns to Na (its Mulliken charge\index{Mulliken charges}) is $+0.95$, so the ion keeps almost all of its charge and shares hardly any electrons with the water. The molecule turns its negative end, the oxygen with its two lone pairs, toward the cation because the positive charge attracts the negative end of the dipole and repels the positive one. Hence the picture of hydrated Na$^+$ in a textbook, with oxygen atoms pointing inward, follows from the attraction of a charge for a dipole alone.

\section{The curve}
The curve answers how strongly one water molecule is held and how far the attraction reaches. Its lowest point is at Na--O \SI{2.21}{\angstrom}, the distance measured in the procedure, \SI{76.8}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated partners (table~\ref{tab:id-curve}; kJ/mol is the energy per mole of pairs). That is thirty times $RT$ at room temperature (\SI{2.5}{kJ.mol^{-1}}), the typical energy that molecules exchange in collisions, so the water molecule that touches the ion stays with it through many collisions. The attraction then falls off quickly: at \SI{4}{\angstrom} only \SI{7.0}{kJ.mol^{-1}} is left, at \SI{5}{\angstrom} \SI{1.8}{kJ.mol^{-1}}, less than $RT$, and at \SI{10}{\angstrom} nothing, the curve lies on the zero line to within a tenth of a kJ/mol. Hence in water an ion holds firmly only the molecules that touch it, which is why a dissolved ion carries a shell of oriented water molecules with it while the water further out moves freely.

\section{Two ions}
The NaCl ion pair shows what changes when the water molecule is replaced by a second ion. Its lowest point is at Na--Cl \SI{2.56}{\angstrom}, longer than the \SI{2.36}{\angstrom} measured in the procedure because that structure is the pair on its own and the water around each ion weakens their pull on each other, and \SI{106.4}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated ions, so at contact the ion pair is held only about 1.4 times as strongly as Na$^+$ with one water molecule. The real difference is in how far the attraction reaches. The attraction between two charges falls off as $R^{-1}$, more slowly than the $R^{-2}$ of a charge and a dipole: at \SI{5}{\angstrom} the ion pair keeps \SI{9.6}{kJ.mol^{-1}}, five times as much as Na$^+\cdots$H$_2$O at the same distance, it stays below $-RT$ out to about \SI{8}{\angstrom}, and at \SI{10}{\angstrom} it still keeps \SI{1.8}{kJ.mol^{-1}} where the ion--dipole curve has nothing left. The dotted reference curve in figure~\ref{fig:id-curve} is Coulomb's law for two whole charges divided by 78.4, the dielectric constant of water, which says how much the water between two charges weakens their attraction; the calculated curve approaches it at large distance, \SI{-1.8}{kJ.mol^{-1}} against \SI{-1.78}{kJ.mol^{-1}} at \SI{10}{\angstrom}, and lies below it at shorter distances, where the ions are too close for much water to fit between them and weaken their pull. Hence two ions in water still feel each other at distances where an ion and a single water molecule no longer do, which is why the ions in a salt solution do not move independently of each other.

\section{History}
Coulomb measured the force between two charged spheres with his torsion balance and reported in 1785 that it falls off as the inverse square of their distance \cite{coulomb1785}; the energy of two ions, $-1389/R$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ for whole charges, follows from his law. Debye introduced the permanent dipole moment of a molecule in 1912 to explain how the dielectric constant of a polar substance depends on temperature \cite{debye1912}, and collected the theory in his book \emph{Polar Molecules} of 1929 \cite{debye1929}. Born treated a hydrated ion in 1920 as a charged sphere in a continuous dielectric and obtained the hydration energy of the ion from its radius and the dielectric constant of water \cite{born1920}; the calculation of this chapter looks at the same attraction one water molecule at a time.
