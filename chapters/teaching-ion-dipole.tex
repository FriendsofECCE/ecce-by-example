\chapter{Ion-dipole interactions}
\label{ch:t-ion-dipole}
\index{Ion--dipole interaction}
\index{Sodium ion}
\index{Intermolecular interactions}
\eccexample{Examples > General Chemistry > Intermolecular interactions}

A sodium ion in water is surrounded by water molecules that turn their oxygen ends toward it. One water molecule and one Na$^+$ is the smallest case of this ion--dipole attraction, and a relaxed scan of the Na--O distance shows how strong it is and how slowly it falls off. The NaCl ion pair of the same topic is the contrast: two ions instead of an ion and a dipole.

\section*{This requires}
Nothing beyond ECCE: the calculations are in the General Chemistry example set.

\section{The calculation}
The topic \menu{Intermolecular interactions} holds \emph{na\_h2o}, one Na$^+$ with one water molecule, and \emph{nacl\_ion\_pair}, both optimised with ECCE-QM at B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP. A relaxed scan of the Na--O and Na--Cl distances (Chapter~\ref{ch:scans}), inward from each optimised structure in steps of \SI{0.1}{\angstrom} and outward to \SI{10}{\angstrom} in steps of \SI{0.2}{\angstrom}, gives the curves of figure~\ref{fig:id-curve}. The zero of each curve is the separated partners, Na$^+$ and H$_2$O, or Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$, each calculated alone with the same method.

\section{Procedure}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item In the Organizer, choose \menu{File > Open Example Calculations}, pick \menu{General Chemistry} and click \menu{OK}.
	\item Open \emph{na\_h2o} in the Viewer. With \menu{Measure} (Chapter~\ref{ch:first-calculation}), measure the distance Na--O and the angles Na--O--H.
	\item Do the same for \emph{nacl\_ion\_pair}, and open \menu{Density Profile} with the line from Na1 to Cl2.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Orientation}
The sodium ion sits on the twofold axis of the water molecule, on the oxygen side, \SI{2.21}{\angstrom} from O. Both hydrogens point away from it, with Na--O--H \ang{127.4}. The molecule turns its negative end, the oxygen with its two lone pairs, toward the cation. The Mulliken charge on Na is $+0.95$, so the ion keeps almost all of its charge.

\section{The curve}
The lowest point of the Na$^+\cdots$H$_2$O curve is at Na--O \SI{2.21}{\angstrom}, \SI{113.2}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated partners (table~\ref{tab:id-curve}), more than four times the depth of the hydrogen bond in the water dimer (Chapter~\ref{sec:hydrogen-bond}). The attraction falls off as $R^{-2}$ and does not level off within \SI{10}{\angstrom}, where the pair is still \SI{6.0}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated partners. A Morse curve, whose attraction decays exponentially, does not describe such a tail, and none is fitted. The energies are not counterpoise-corrected (ECCE issue 1091).

\section{Two ions}
The NaCl ion pair has its lowest point at Na--Cl \SI{2.36}{\angstrom}, \SI{591.5}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the separated ions, five times the ion--dipole well. The attraction between two charges falls off as $R^{-1}$, more slowly than the $R^{-2}$ of the ion--dipole case: the dotted reference curve in figure~\ref{fig:id-curve}, the Coulomb energy $-1389/R$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ ($R$ in \AA) of two whole charges, is still \SI{-139}{kJ.mol^{-1}} at \SI{10}{\angstrom}.

The computed curve ends \SI{241}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below its zero, and the reason is in how the two are calculated. The zero is two separate calculations, Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$, each with a whole charge by construction. The curve is one calculation of the pair, and at \SI{9.96}{\angstrom} its stored Mulliken charges are $\pm0.56$: it ends in a state that is neither two ions nor two atoms, and so heads for a different limit from the line it is drawn against. The gap is the difference between these two descriptions, not an attraction; the separately computed neutral atoms lie \SI{199}{kJ.mol^{-1}} below the ions, and the curve ends below both lines.
\index{Ion pair}\index{Mulliken charges}

Along the line from Na1 to Cl2 (figure~\ref{fig:id-profile}) the total density falls to 0.0351~e/bohr$^3$ between the ions, 0.98~\AA\ from Na1.
